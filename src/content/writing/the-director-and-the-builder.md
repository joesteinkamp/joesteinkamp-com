For most of my career, design leadership meant knowing what good looks like and making sure the team gets there. Brief, review, feedback loop. The design leader as informed observer.

AI is changing the calculus. Not because it makes design leaders obsolete — it doesn't — but because it makes building fast enough that the director/maker split no longer has to be permanent.

## The director mode

The traditional design leader's leverage is taste. You can't review what you can't evaluate, so developing the right instincts — about interaction, hierarchy, tone — is the core job. You get there by making things yourself, early in your career, and then you extend that taste to a team.

The problem is the feedback loop. By the time a brief becomes a design becomes a prototype becomes a build, weeks have passed and the original judgment call is buried under layers of interpretation. The director makes a decision about the direction of a river from a satellite photo.

## The builder mode

Building means making something that works — not a spec, not a mockup, not a handoff artifact. AI has compressed the time between "here's the idea" and "here's the thing" enough that this is now realistic for people who couldn't previously build their way there.

What's different when you build?

1. **You discover constraints you'd have missed.** Describing a UI pattern and implementing one are not the same problem. The implementation reveals edge cases, performance considerations, and platform quirks that no brief captures.

2. **Your taste calibrates differently.** Reviewing a design, you evaluate against your best mental model. Building, you encounter the actual tradeoffs. The feedback is immediate and unambiguous: it either works or it doesn't.

3. **Quality decisions compound.** In a review cycle, each feedback round costs a person-day. In a build cycle, the same decision costs seconds. You can make 10x more taste decisions in the same time, and taste — like any skill — improves through repetition.

## What this doesn't mean

Building isn't better than directing for all work. A design leader who only builds is a solo contributor — the leverage is gone. The value is in oscillating: making enough to keep the taste calibration sharp, directing enough to extend that calibration to a team.

It also doesn't mean the skills are the same. There's real craft in building with AI that isn't automatic. You still have to know what you're trying to make before you can get there; the tool doesn't substitute for judgment, it just moves faster once you have it.

## The open question

What changes about design education when building is accessible earlier? The old path was: make things until your taste is good, then extend your taste to others. If you can now build meaningful things with less technical fluency, does the taste calibration happen faster — or does it atrophy because you never encounter the full cost of your decisions?

I don't have an answer yet. But it seems like the right question.
