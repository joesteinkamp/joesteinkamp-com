For most of my career, "design leadership" meant directing — setting direction,
managing craft, bridging design and product. You shaped things through people and
process. Shipping was someone else's job.

AI has changed that boundary. In the past few months I've shipped a natural-language
GUI library, a Chrome extension for screenshotting entire web apps, an AI skills
library for designers, and a visual webpage editor that lives in the browser. None
of these are demo projects. They're tools I actually use, or that I built because
I wanted them to exist and no one else was going to build them fast enough.

The change isn't that AI writes code for me — it's that the bottleneck moved. The
hard part of building used to be the implementation. It isn't anymore. The hard
part now is knowing what to build, why it should exist, and what it should feel
like. Those are questions I spent the last decade learning to answer.

Design fluency transfers pretty well to AI-assisted engineering. Not because AI
makes everyone a programmer, but because knowing what a piece of software should
do — from the outside in — is still rare and still matters. The muscle I developed
over years of product design work is precisely what determines whether the thing
you're building with AI is worth building at all.

A few patterns I keep running into:

**The gap between "I can prompt this" and "I know what to build" is wider than it
looks.** Generating code is easy. Deciding which abstraction to reach for, what the
usage surface should feel like, which edge cases matter — those decisions come from
design thinking applied to systems design.

**Interface-adjacent tools are underbuilt.** Chrome extensions, design tooling, UI
libraries for AI apps — these are areas where design leaders have high domain
fluency but historically had to wait for engineers to prioritize the work. That
wait is largely over.

**The interesting work is now framing problems, not implementing solutions.** Writing
a good brief for an AI agent isn't that different from writing a good design brief.
The deliverable is different; the skill is similar.

I'm still figuring out what design leadership looks like when building is on the
table. But I suspect the answer isn't "design leaders become engineers." It's
closer to: the edges of what design leadership can mean are expanding, and the
people who invest in understanding those edges now will have a real advantage in a
few years.
