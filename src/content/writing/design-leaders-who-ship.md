There's a version of design leadership that is almost entirely verbal. You define the vision. You set the standards. You review the work. You coach the team. It's important work — but it is also, if you're honest about it, work that keeps you at a remove from the actual making.

That felt reasonable when making was hard. When shipping a feature meant months of coordination across design, engineering, and product, a design leader's job was to make that coordination work. The ratio made sense: one leader's judgment applied across many engineers' output.

AI coding agents broke that ratio.

Now a single design leader, fluent in what they want, can build a working product in the time it used to take to write the spec. Not a prototype. Not a mock-up. A thing that runs, deploys, and can be handed to a real user. The coordination overhead that made building impractical — the stand-ups, the back-and-forth, the review cycles — compresses down to prompts and a pull request.

Which creates a genuinely new question: why wouldn't you build?

---

The honest answer is that most design leaders don't have the instinct yet. Not because they couldn't learn the tools, but because the reflex was trained out of them. Leadership, over time, came to mean not doing the thing yourself. Delegation became the proof of competence. The design leader who still got into pixels was suspect — surely struggling to let go.

Agents dissolve that logic. If you can write a brief that turns into production code in a few hours, the question of whether you should write that code yourself is moot. The work *is* the brief. The craft is in knowing what to ask for, how to evaluate it, and when to trust the output.

Design leaders are, it turns out, extremely well-positioned for that. The pattern recognition built over a career — across products, users, systems, and failure modes — is exactly what writing a good agent brief requires. The skills transferred. They just weren't pointed at the right problem before.

---

What this actually looks like in practice: you stop waiting. An idea for how a feature should work becomes a repo by afternoon. A tool your team needs but will never get prioritized in the eng backlog, you build yourself in a week. You can test real hypotheses with real implementations, not with Figma prototypes that lie about interaction cost and load time.

The constraint isn't skill anymore. It's judgment about what's worth building.

That's a constraint design leaders are supposed to be good at. It's the whole job.

The reflex to direct rather than build — to manage rather than make — was a rational adaptation to a world where making was expensive. That world is gone. The question now is whether design leaders will adapt as fast as the tools did.
